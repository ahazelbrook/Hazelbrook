A first published cost and a current cost are almost never the same number, and the difference is usually not a mistake. Four things move between them: **scope** — what the project now includes; **timing** — whether the dollars are today's or the ones that will actually be spent; **risk allocation** — who carries what, and therefore who has to price it; and **contingency basis** — whether the figure is a P50 or a P90. A cost quoted without those four cannot be compared with anything. Most reporting on infrastructure costs sets a number struck on one basis against a number struck on another, and calls the difference a blowout.

## Why do two official numbers disagree with each other?

Often because the business case was required to contain both.

Under Queensland's Business Case Development Framework, project costs are presented in real, P50 terms in the economic appraisal chapter and in P90 terms in the financial analysis chapter — [the framework's cost–benefit analysis guide says so directly](https://www.statedevelopment.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0013/55030/further-guidance-04-cost-benefit-analysis-guide.pdf), on the reasoning that P50 is the more appropriate comparative indicator for a benefit–cost ratio. Other jurisdictions arrange it differently, but the pattern holds: the number used to test whether a project is worth doing is not the number used to decide how much money to set aside for it.

So a reader who takes the appraisal figure out of one document and sets it against a funding announcement made five years later has compared a real P50 with a nominal P90. The gap between those two can be tens of percentage points on a project where nothing has gone wrong at all.

## What do P50 and P90 mean?

A modern capital cost estimate is a distribution. The base estimate and the risk register are run through thousands of simulated outcomes with ranges on the inputs. What comes out is a curve, and P-values are points on it.

P50 and P90 are, in the Commonwealth's own words, "the costs with sufficient contingency to provide a 50% and 90% likelihood respectively that these costs will not be exceeded" — the definition set out in the Infrastructure Investment Program's [guidance note on probabilistic contingency estimation](https://investment.infrastructure.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/guidance-note-3A-probabilistic-cost-estimation-v2.pdf), which makes probabilistic estimation mandatory for any project seeking Australian Government funding with a total anticipated outturn cost above $25 million.

Two consequences follow, and both are routinely missed.

**A P50 is expected to be exceeded half the time.** That is what the number means. It is the median, not the ceiling and not a promise. A program delivered consistently at its P50 estimates is a program working exactly as designed, and roughly half of it will still report a cost above the published figure. Treating a P50 as a commitment and then reporting the overrun is a category error, not a finding.

**A P90 is not the worst case either.** One project in ten is expected to land above it. P90 exists because a funder holding a portfolio needs to know what to reserve, not because anyone believes it is what the project will cost.

The distance between the two is itself worth reading. A wide P50–P90 spread says the risk is not yet resolved: ground conditions unknown, utilities unmapped, approvals unsettled, market untested. A narrow spread says the project has been defined. When an agency's spread narrows between business case stages, that is progress, even though the P90 may have gone up.

## Are these dollars from the same year?

This is the distinction that decides most long-program arguments, and it is invisible in a headline.

A **real** cost is expressed in the dollars of a stated base date — today's money. An **outturn**, or **nominal**, cost is the same project with escalation applied across the years in which the money will actually be spent; the Commonwealth's [escalation guidance](https://investment.infrastructure.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/guidance-note-4-escalation-v3.pdf) treats a cost that includes escalation as an outturn cost, escalated from the estimate's base date to completion of construction.

Over a two-year project the difference is a rounding error. Over a decade it is the whole argument.

> Nothing about the project has changed — only which year's dollars you are counting in.

And the escalation is not hypothetical. Infrastructure Australia's [2024 Infrastructure Market Capacity Report](https://www.infrastructureaustralia.gov.au/reports/2024-infrastructure-market-capacity-report) found the cost of land transport infrastructure construction had risen 51–53% since 2010–11, with as much growth in the three years to that report as in the preceding ten. An estimate struck in 2019 real dollars, restated as outturn for a build finishing in 2030, will be a substantially larger number before a single line of scope moves. The agency that publishes the larger figure is not confessing to anything. It is telling you what the cash requirement is.

## How much scope moved between the two numbers?

Usually more than anyone remembers, because early numbers are struck against early scope.

The classification most estimators work from is explicit about this. Under [AACE International's cost estimate classification system](https://services.austintexas.gov/edims/document.cfm?id=280770), a Class 5 estimate — concept screening, prepared when the project is 0 to 2% defined — carries an expected accuracy range as wide as −50 to +100%. That is not a licence for sloppiness. It is an honest statement of what can be known before anyone has drilled a borehole.

**An illustration, invented for this article — the figures are not from a real project.** Take a suburban rail extension:

- **Strategic assessment.** Twelve kilometres of new track and four stations, on a corridor already in a network plan. Order-of-magnitude estimate: **$2.4bn**, real, P50.
- **Options analysis.** The preferred option needs two grade separations, because the level crossings on the corridor cannot carry the service frequency the case assumes. It needs a stabling facility, because the timetable has to berth trains somewhere overnight. Property acquisition rises as the alignment is fixed to a surveyed line rather than a corridor. **$3.1bn**, real, P50.
- **Detailed business case.** Geotechnical investigation finds one station has to be rebuilt rather than modified. Signalling and power on the connecting line have to be upgraded to deliver the frequency the benefits depend on. **$3.8bn**, real, P50 — and, escalated across a seven-year build and taken to P90 for the funding decision, **$4.9bn** outturn.

The headline writes itself: *$2.4bn project now $4.9bn*. Every figure is correct. None of them is comparable to any other. And the largest single item in that progression — the signalling upgrade — is there because without it the benefits do not arrive, which is a point about the case rather than about the cost. The same discipline applies on the other side of the ledger; we have written about [what kills a BCR at assurance](/journal/what-kills-a-bcr-at-assurance/) separately.

## Who is carrying the risk in each number?

The same physical project prices differently under different commercial structures, and not because the concrete costs more.

When an owner retains ground risk, the contract sum is lower and the contingency sits on the owner's side of the line — sometimes inside the published figure, sometimes not. When that risk transfers, the contractor prices it, adds a margin for holding it, and the contract sum absorbs the lot. Australia's recent road and rail programs have moved decisively toward incentivised target cost and alliance-style arrangements, in which cost movement is shared rather than transferred outright. That changes what a headline number even represents.

So a figure from a design-and-construct era and a figure from a shared-risk era are not two measurements of the same quantity. Reconciling them onto a consistent basis is ordinary [assurance work](/#services), and it is the first thing a reviewer does before forming any view about whether a project has gone off the rails.

## So how much of the gap is error?

Most of it usually is not — but not all of it, and the evidence about which part is which is unflattering to a different party than the reporting implies.

The Grattan Institute's study of [cost overruns in transport infrastructure](https://grattan.edu.au/report/cost-overruns-in-transport-infrastructure/) examined every project of $20 million or more planned or built since 2001 — 836 of them — and found actual costs exceeded promised costs by 24%. The finding that matters is the distribution. Projects announced early, before a funding commitment, made up 32% of the sample and accounted for 74% of the value of the overruns.

Read that carefully. The variable that predicts an overrun is *when the number was first said out loud*, not who delivered the project. A cost announced before a business case exists is a cost produced without the information that would make it right, and the agency that inherits it spends the next five years explaining a figure it did not set. The 2023 [Independent Strategic Review of the Infrastructure Investment Program](https://investment.infrastructure.gov.au/about/budget-announcements/infrastructure-investment-program-strategic-review) reached the same territory from the other direction, identifying more than $32.8bn of cost pressure across the Commonwealth pipeline and pointing at escalation, thin early costings and premature commitment rather than at delivery.

An estimate is a statement about what is not yet known, and it gets better as that shrinks.

## Before comparing any two numbers

**Scope.** Same stations, same track, same signalling, same land, same operating readiness — if the second number bought more project, it should be larger.

**Dollars.** Real at what base date, or outturn escalated to which completion year. Never compare across that line without restating one of them.

**Confidence.** P50, P90 or an expected value, and out of which chapter of which document.

**Risk.** Owner-retained contingency and a transferred, priced allocation produce different headline figures for identical work.

Ask those four and most alleged blowouts resolve into something duller and more useful: a project whose cost is now known better than it was. What is left after that — and there is usually something left — is the part worth arguing about. Separating the two is a large share of [what we do](/#practice).
