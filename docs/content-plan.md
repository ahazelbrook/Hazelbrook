# Content & search plan — the Journal

How Hazelbrook earns search visibility, and the brief for the first four
articles. Voice is governed by [`copy-plan.md`](copy-plan.md) §4 — this document
does not restate it, and nothing here overrides it.

---

## 1 · What we can and cannot know

**There is no keyword tool in this project.** No Ahrefs, no SEMrush, no Search
Console. Every "volume" figure in a plan built without those is invented, so
there are none here. What follows is grounded in what actually ranks for these
queries today, what the government and law-firm sources publish, and where the
gaps sit.

Two consequences:

1. Priorities below are **judgment, labelled as such**. Once the site is live,
   connect Search Console and replace this section with measured data. The
   queries that actually bring people in are rarely the ones anyone predicted.
2. Success is measured on **enquiries, not rankings**. For a practice taking a
   small number of engagements, one government reader who arrives via a BCR
   article and sends a note is worth more than a thousand sessions.

## 2 · The strategy, and why it is not the obvious one

**Do not chase the head terms.** "Infrastructure advisory Melbourne",
"infrastructure consultants Australia" and similar are held by KPMG, EY, HKA and
E3 Advisory — firms with a decade of domain authority and a hundred pages each.
A new domain entering that auction loses, slowly and expensively.

**Compete where expertise beats authority.** The opening is long-tail,
high-intent, practitioner-grade questions where the existing results are one of:

- **Government primary sources** — Infrastructure Australia, ATAP, state
  treasuries. Authoritative, thorough, and written as compliance documents. They
  answer *what the rule is*. They never answer *what actually happens*.
- **Law firm briefings** — accurate, narrow, written for lawyers, and structured
  around legal risk rather than commercial consequence.
- **Nothing at all.**

A practitioner who has been on both sides of these numbers can write the missing
third thing. That is a defensible position that no amount of competitor spend
displaces.

**The AI-search consequence.** A growing share of this audience — particularly
government readers doing preliminary research — now asks an assistant rather
than scrolling results. Assistants synthesise from sources that state specifics
plainly: real figures, named mechanisms, clear definitions, dates. That reward
structure favours exactly the content described above, and it is a second reason
to be concrete rather than promotional. **Practical rule: every article must
contain at least three specific, checkable, attributed facts.** Vague thought
leadership is invisible to both a search engine and an assistant.

## 3 · On-page requirements

Non-negotiable for every article. The prerender script enforces the mechanical
ones; the writer owns the rest.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| **URL** | `/journal/<slug>/` — short, hyphenated, keyword-bearing, never dated |
| **Title tag** | ≤ 60 characters. Front-load the query. Suffix ` — Hazelbrook & Hazelbrook` is added automatically, so the frontmatter title must fit inside the remaining budget |
| **Meta description** | 140–155 characters. Written to earn a click, not to repeat the title |
| **H1** | Exactly one, matching the article's actual argument. May differ from the title tag |
| **H2/H3** | Real questions where a reader would ask one. These become the featured-snippet and assistant-citation surface |
| **First 100 words** | State the answer. Do not warm up. A reader who leaves after two sentences should still have got the thing they searched for |
| **Specifics** | ≥ 3 checkable facts with sources, linked externally |
| **Internal links** | ≥ 2 — one to the relevant service area on the home page, one to another article once the batch exists |
| **JSON-LD** | `Article` schema, generated by the build from frontmatter |
| **Length** | 1,200–1,800 words. Long enough to be the best answer, short enough to be read |

**On external links:** link generously to Infrastructure Australia, ATAP and
academic sources. Linking to primary sources is what a confident practitioner
does, and hoarding link equity is a 2012 concern that costs credibility now.

### 4.1 · Why patronage forecasts miss

- **Slug:** `why-patronage-forecasts-miss`
- **Target cluster:** traffic forecast accuracy Australia · patronage forecast
  optimism bias · why toll road traffic forecasts are wrong · demand forecasting
  error transport
- **Priority:** 1. Strongest piece on the list — evidenced, underserved, and the
  single best demonstration of the practice's judgement.
- **Maps to:** Service 02, Demand & Network Evidence.

**Angle.** The error is *systematic and asymmetric*, not incompetence — that is
the whole argument. Forecasts miss high far more often than low, which tells you
the cause is structural rather than random. Explain the mechanism (the value of
travel-time savings is estimated in a way that biases upward; ramp-up is
optimistic; the forecast is produced inside a process that wants the project to
proceed), then give the reader what to actually do about it: the questions that
interrogate a forecast.

**Must contain:**
- Toll road demand averages **~77% of forecast** — roughly 23% below — against
  ~96% for untolled roads, and the asymmetry of the error distribution.
  ([Transport Reviews](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01441640903211173))
- **Sydney's Cross City Tunnel opened at roughly one third of forecast traffic.**
  The most famous Australian instance, and it must be handled under the copy
  plan's candour test — explain the mechanism, do not score the point.
- Year-one forecasts run **20–30% above actual** across ~87 studied projects.
- Optimism bias as a named, documented phenomenon in transport appraisal, not an
  accusation. ([Frontier Economics](https://frontier-economics.com.au/publications/expecting-the-unexpected-the-challenges-of-traffic-forecasting))

**Ends on:** what a reader should ask of the next forecast they are handed.
Practical, numbered, and genuinely useful even to someone who never calls us.

### 4.2 · Incentivised Target Cost, explained

- **Slug:** `incentivised-target-cost-explained`
- **Target cluster:** incentivised target cost contract · ITC contract Australia
  · incentivised target cost vs alliance · collaborative contracting Australia ·
  target cost contract infrastructure
- **Priority:** 2. The best pure-search opportunity on the list: real and rising
  demand, and the only existing supply is law firms writing for lawyers.
- **Maps to:** Service 04, Transactions & Close.

**Angle.** A plain-English explanation of a model that is quietly becoming the
Australian default, written for the commercial reader rather than the legal one.
What it is, how the pain/gain share actually works, what it fixes about D&C, what
it borrows from alliancing — and, the part nobody writes, **where it still
leaves risk**. An explainer that only explains is a brochure.

**Must contain:**
- ITC as a hybrid of D&C and alliance, developed in response to pricing
  volatility, with cost savings and overruns shared between the parties.
- **Victoria:** the model of choice for road delivery; used inside the PPP
  structure for North East Link's design-and-construct phase, and on Suburban
  Rail Loop's tunnelling package.
- **NSW:** Sydney Metro, Warringah Freeway Upgrade, Western Harbour Tunnel.
- The **High Speed Rail Authority's** delivery strategy deliberately avoiding a
  single mega-PPP in favour of multiple packages with early contractor
  involvement and ITC.
  ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/analysis/australian-transmission-projects-target-cost-contracts-risks))
- Honest treatment of the open question: a target cost is only as good as the
  estimate it is set from, and setting it early — before design maturity —
  moves risk back to the owner regardless of what the pain share says.

**Ends on:** when ITC is the right instrument and when it is not.

### 4.3 · How to read an infrastructure cost number

- **Slug:** `how-to-read-an-infrastructure-cost-number`
- **Target cluster:** why infrastructure projects cost more than estimated ·
  infrastructure cost blowout explained Australia · P50 P90 infrastructure cost
  estimate · project cost escalation Australia
- **Priority:** 3. The most differentiating piece and the most linkable; also
  the least directly commercial, which is why it sits third rather than first.
- **Maps to:** the practice section, and the track record's Cross River Rail card.

**Angle.** Expand the argument already on the home page: a first published cost
and a current cost are almost never the same thing. Four things move between
them — **scope, timing (nominal vs real dollars), risk allocation, and
contingency basis (P50 vs P90)** — and a number quoted without those four is not
information. Written so a journalist, a board member or a new analyst can read
the next headline properly.

**Must contain:**
- The nominal-versus-real distinction and why a decade-long program makes it
  decisive.
- P50 versus P90 contingency, plainly explained — the single most common reason
  two honest numbers for the same project differ.
- A worked, neutral illustration of scope change across business case stages.
- Explicit statement that most of the gap is usually **not** error. This is what
  makes the piece credible to the agencies who are its readers, and it passes the
  copy plan's candour test by construction.

**Ends on:** the four questions to ask before comparing any two infrastructure
numbers.

### 4.4 · What kills a BCR at assurance

- **Slug:** `what-kills-a-bcr-at-assurance`
- **Target cluster:** benefit cost ratio business case Australia · BCR
  sensitivity testing · discount rate infrastructure appraisal Australia ·
  business case assurance review · cost benefit analysis infrastructure Australia
- **Priority:** 4. The most contested territory — Infrastructure Australia and
  ATAP own the definitional queries — but the highest buyer intent on the list.
  Someone searching this is writing a business case right now.
- **Maps to:** Service 01, Strategy, Business Case & Assurance.

**Angle.** Do not explain what a BCR is; that ground is taken and holding it is
not worth the effort. Write the piece that only a reviewer can write: **the
assumptions that fail scrutiny**, in the order they fail. The home page already
claims that cases fail on assumptions rather than arithmetic — this article is
the evidence for that claim.

**Must contain:**
- The **7% real discount rate** convention, and the requirement to present
  **3%, 7% and 10%** in detailed analysis — and why a case whose conclusion
  flips between them has a problem the BCR is not showing.
  ([IA guide to economic appraisal](https://www.infrastructureaustralia.gov.au/sites/default/files/2021-07/Assessment%20Framework%202021%20Guide%20to%20economic%20appraisal.pdf))
- **20 or 30 year** appraisal periods, and the sensitivity of the result to
  residual value assumptions at the boundary.
- Infrastructure Australia's framework recommending **at least two options plus
  a base case** — and that a weak base case, not a weak option, is the most
  common fatal flaw. ([IA Assessment Framework](https://www.infrastructureaustralia.gov.au/publications/assessment-framework))
- The demand forecast as the input everything else rests on — **internally
  linked to 4.1**.
- That BCR is meant to sit alongside qualitative and quantitative evidence in an
  integrated assessment, not to be a single score to be maximised.

**Ends on:** what a reviewer looks at first, which is also a quiet demonstration
of what we do.

## 5 · Publishing mechanics

- Articles are **markdown files** in `src/journal/`, with YAML frontmatter.
  Prose belongs in prose files, not TypeScript arrays.
- `scripts/prerender.mjs` runs after `vite build` and emits real, static,
  no-JavaScript-required HTML for every article, plus the journal index,
  `sitemap.xml` and `robots.txt`.
- Articles carry **no client-side JavaScript at all**. They are prose; they need
  none, and a page that renders without JS is the most reliably indexable thing
  that exists.
- The journal is linked from the site header so it is reachable, and every
  article links back into the relevant service area.
